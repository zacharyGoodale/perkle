# Benefit-credit versus refund risk: category priors and amount signals

Research date: October 30, 2026. Sources are first-party merchant/issuer policies, government guidance, and primary industry research. This note supplies evidence and proposed product policy, not a measured population refund rate or a recommendation about which financial product to buy. No paid calls or production changes were made.

## Conclusion

**Benefit-specific risk treatment makes sense; the claim that dining-related refunds are extremely rare is not established by the primary evidence found.** Physical-goods returns and travel cancellations are explicit, well-supported sources of confounding credits. Dining and subscriptions plausibly have fewer routine return opportunities, but missing items, service failures, billing corrections, and cancellations still produce refunds. I did not find a credible primary dataset estimating the relevant frequency for this cardholder population.

Use exact benefit amount as **supporting evidence combined with merchant/program description and the actual card**, not as a stand-alone automatic-match rule. Use affirmative refund/adjustment evidence to reject a match rather than treating any hypothetical refund possibility as a veto. Preserve review for unresolved cases. The current personal dataset is useful for counterexamples and prompt development; it cannot establish how rare false attribution will be for other users.

## What the evidence can quantify

NRF and Happy Returns estimated that **15.8% of annual retail sales would be returned in 2025**, representing $849.9 billion. This is merchandise/retail sales value, not a probability that a transaction is a refund, not a restaurant rate, and not the share of negative transactions that are refunds. The survey population also should not be treated as representative of card-benefit users. [NRF primary release](https://cdn.nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-expected-to-return-nearly-850-billion-in-merchandise-in-2025)

Bank of America's own card data analysis finds particularly high returns at department stores and differences by household income and generation. That supports taking shopping refunds seriously, especially for the Saks/Nordstrom-type comparison. It does not estimate refund-versus-benefit confusion among negative credit-card transactions. [Bank of America Institute, August 2025](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/economic-insights/retail-returns.html)

There is a denominator problem: the quantity needed for this product is approximately `P(unrelated refund | negative transaction, actual card, merchant/program, amount, timing)`. An overall return rate among purchases or refunded sales dollars does not supply that probability. A rare event among all purchases may still form a substantial share of the small pool of negative transactions.

## Amount matching: useful, conditional, and date-sensitive

1. **Compare against the correct cap.** Issuer advertising often gives an annual total while credits reset monthly, quarterly, semiannually, or by cardmember year. For example, Gold Resy is up to $50 per half-year, not a $100-per-transaction benefit. Chase dining and StubHub are accumulated limits per half-year. [Gold Resy terms](https://www.americanexpress.com/en-us/account/get-started/gold/explore-benefits), [Chase Reserve terms](https://creditcards.chase.com/rewards-credit-cards/sapphire/reserve)
2. **Track remaining cap, not only full advertised cap.** A reimbursement can equal the eligible purchase amount or the unconsumed allowance. Partial amounts and multiple credits are normal under accumulated caps. A mismatch with the full cap is not evidence against a benefit.
3. **Exact-cap refunds are possible.** A full refund of a fixed-price membership can have exactly the same amount as its issuer reimbursement. CLEAR's policy supplies an actual mechanism. Round merchant credits, price adjustments, and unrelated benefits can also collide; frequency is unknown. [CLEAR refund policy](https://www.clearme.com/support/what-is-your-refund-policy)
4. **The $200 airline intuition is promising but uncalibrated.** Reimbursement may be clipped to the remaining $200 annual allowance. But the relevant comparison is other negative airline transactions, including incidental-fee refunds, not the distribution of ticket purchase prices. [Amex airline terms](https://www.americanexpress.com/en-us/travel/faq/travel-benefits/), [DOT ancillary-fee guidance](https://www.transportation.gov/individuals/aviation-consumer-protection/refunds)
5. **Version caps by effective date.** The project configuration still lists $20/month for Platinum digital entertainment while the current official page says $25. The hotel benefit changed from $200/year to $300 semiannually effective September 18, 2025. A history spanning 2024–2026 must not be scored against a single current catalog cap. [Current Platinum page](https://www.americanexpress.com/us/credit-cards/card/platinum/), [Official hotel change announcement](https://www.americanexpress.com/en-us/travel/get-inspired/platinum-card-600-dollar-hotel-credit/)

Reasonable features are `matches_historically_applicable_period_cap`, `matches_remaining_cap_if_known`, `amount_at_or_below_cap`, and `cap_version_known`, computed in code with cent-exact arithmetic. Treat them as inputs whose value must be tested, not likelihood ratios invented from intuition. A positive exact match can raise confidence; lack of one should not force a non-match.

## A separate risk: correct credits can later be reversed

Both Amex and Chase warn that issuer statement credits may be reversed after qualifying purchases are returned or canceled. Therefore the ledger can become wrong even if the original classifier was right. A negative-only benefit detector does not by itself handle a later positive issuer clawback. Preserve transaction lineage and provide a separate reversal/reconciliation or manual-correction path. [Amex airline terms](https://www.americanexpress.com/en-us/travel/faq/travel-benefits/), [Chase Reserve terms](https://creditcards.chase.com/rewards-credit-cards/sapphire/reserve)

## Suggested risk policy

- **Be explicit about exclusion evidence:** literal refund/return/reversal language, known payments/split transfers, or a specifically identified internal redistribution with an offsetting entry. The vague possibility of an adjustment should not defeat a clearly named issuer benefit credit.
- **Use category and cap as priors, not verdicts:** stronger semantic evidence and verified historical amount fit can justify more permissive handling for some benefits. Shopping and opaque travel cases deserve more review. Exact cap plus only merchant identity remains weaker than explicit issuer-credit wording.
- **Measure accepted risk:** false automatic matches among randomly audited auto-actions, by benefit category and amount-fit group; missed credits and review burden separately. User correction feedback alone is selection-biased, and recurring descriptors are not independent evidence.
- **Choose thresholds against asymmetric costs:** compare expected benefit value lost through false auto-completion with missed-credit inconvenience/extra spending and review cost. A model's uncalibrated 0.90 probability does not directly establish a 10% error risk. Numerical category-specific thresholds require additional labeled data.
- **Use a smarter fallback to interpret evidence, not invent it:** it may recognize equivalent issuer wording, consider the cap, or identify an adjustment pair. It cannot establish a merchant-only credit's origin when the source does not contain it. Keep a review outcome for such cases.

The defensible product conclusion is to narrow the refund exclusion and test cap-aware features, while accepting that any proposal to tolerate a supposedly “very rare” false match still needs a measured rate. Public refund policies and aggregate retail statistics do not supply that rate.
